## Getting nothing done

I got nothing done this week, and therefore have nothing to write about in my blog.

![Tumbleweed](https://media.giphy.com/media/Az1CJ2MEjmsp2/giphy.gif)

That would be a pretty bad blog post, for several reasons, but it’s a post which is unfortunately more accurate than I would like to admit. I would love to be able to write endless paragraphs about the successes of this week, and how I’ve progressed in leaps and bounds since last week but there really isn’t that much to say when it comes to actual objective successes this week for me. This blog isn’t meant to just be about everything that’s worked during our project, it’s meant to help guide you, the reader, along this journey with us, to share the highs and the lows. So, despite that fact it seems strange to be writing about my failures and difficulties as part of a uni assignment, here we go.

The biggest problem from this week has been the Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) Mesh Network for the ESP32, our microcontroller of choice for this project. As I’ve mentioned a lot over the last few weeks, our hope is to use BLE Mesh to help the glowsticks and speakers or hubs in our system communicate. BLE uses much less power than traditional Bluetooth and many other types of wireless communication, and a mech topology enables it to span distances beyond the range that could be achieved with a more conventional topology. It all sounds like very cool and powerful tech; so much to that SIG (the group behind Bluetooth) have released an on-demand webinar that tells the “behind the scenes story of the making of Bluetooth mesh” called What makes Bluetooth Mesh so Disruptive? (really).

So why then has this not been a week of excitement, disruption and progress? Well, the answer to that is that the software we’re using for BLE Mesh is still very new. Although neither Bluetooth Low Energy nor mesh networks are very new technology, the combination of the two is quite recent, only being released in mid 2017. As much as the tech world likes to talk about moving fast, much of what the tech world relies on is older, tested technology. Next year Java will celebrate its 25th birthday and yet it remains one of, if not the most popular programming language today. One of the reasons for this popularity is undoubtedly the huge community that surrounds Java, which makes it much easier to resolve issues compared to languages that have a smaller community or which are still new and lack adequate documentation or are just plain buggy. And this is essentially the problem I’ve been having with the BLE mesh code; despite being cutting-edge and capable of some really cool stuff, it’s been very difficult to figure out how to actually make it work. As far as I can tell, the API Reference documents haven’t been updated to mention BLE mesh, and apart from the single demo provided by Espressif themselves, there isn’t much working code available to study to try and figure out how the BLE mesh works for the ESP-IDF. Instead, what I’ve been forced to do is to read through the code that comprises the BLE mesh framework, and step though it line-by-line trying to understand what’s being done and why. Often this involves searching my computer for a variable name and then searching all the files that contain that variable name until I find it’s definition.

Despite the painstakingly slow progress, I do feel as if I’m closer to cracking the BLE mesh compared to last week. I do genuinely believe that over the coming weekend I could have this working as we need it to be, however this all sounds eerily familiar to my last post in which I also thought the BLE mesh would be working within a few days. Clearly something needs to change.

In the world of mountaineering, the concept of a turnback time is well established. As the time implies, a turnback time is a pre-determined time in which a climber will turn around and head home (or at least back towards safety) regardless of how far they may have come, or how close they are to their goal. There are countless stories of climbers getting within just a few hundred metres of the summit of Mt. Everest only to hit their turnaround time and simply have to turn away from their goal and head back down the mountain. But there are of course many other examples of climbers ignoring their turnback times and having their climbs end in tragedy.

I think the solution to my BLE mesh problem is implementing a turnback time for BLE mesh. The reality is that while BLE mesh has great potential, it isn’t the only technology that could power our IoT project. I am afraid that without setting some kind of limit, we will continue to throw time at the goal of getting BLE mesh working while ignoring the rest of the project, instead of realising when the BLE mesh has become too resource intensive and adapting our plans to use another technology.

As such, I think that if by Friday one week from now the BLE mesh code isn’t working at a level that is at least similar to what is needed for out project, I’ll consider it time to “turnback” and investigate some of the more established options for wireless communication. Not only do I think this will help prevent spending too much time on this one part of the project, I also think having a firm deadline may help motivate us to get this down.

While this week wasn’t as successful as I had hoped, I did get some things done this week. In additional to the progress made towards BLE mesh, I also met up with my project partner twice this week. Although this isn’t ground-breaking, I think having regular meetings has really helped keep us both on the same page and bouncing ideas around has helped us sure up some of the details of the project. Additionally, I spent longer than I would like to admit getting my GIF uploaded correctly into my previous blog post. Although this was theoretically an easy task, it was made much more complicated by different image hosting sites having different formats for making GIFs available. Imgur for example would only allow me to link to my GIF as an MP4, which can’t be embedded in image tags. Giphy does let you have a links to the .GIF file, but my upload to Giphy kept failing. Eventually, after many, many attempts I managed to get the image uploaded there and linked correctly so that it would display on my blog page.
