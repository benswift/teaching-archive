## The Kylie Jenner of BLE Mesh on the ESP32

If the name Kylie Jenner doesn’t ring a bell, apparently (like me) you have some catching up to do. According to a quick Google search, Kylie Jenner is the most famous person of 2019 (at least based on her status as an Instagram “influencer”). Without sounding obnoxious, in the last week I think Oliver and I have laid the foundations to become the Kylie Jenner of Bluetooth Low Energy Mesh Networking on the ESP32.

It’s true that I don’t think this title is going to carry the same financial benefits as being the world’s most famous person, but it isn’t without its perks. For example, after only a few days of work we successfully have a very rough prototype in which we managed to get an LED strip to blink as the device gets up and running and communicates with the board next to it.

![BLE Mesh LED demo](https://media.giphy.com/media/7vzF9sl5bL0LSQ2Trz/giphy.gif)

Despite seeming pretty insignificant, this actually represents a big step towards completion of our project. In many ways, the code itself and what this code is actually doing doesn’t matter, what matters is that we understand how and why it works. As mentioned in our earlier posts, the software we’re using for Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) Mesh Networking is still in beta, and as we discovered this week, getting started with beta software can be difficult. Going into this part of the project, my biggest concern was that the beta software would be buggy, unstable and generally full of errors and weird, undocumented behaviour. As it turns out, we haven’t encountered any of that (yet) but we have encountered what can be another big concern with beta software – a lack of documentation. As I mentioned in last week’s blog post, when we settled on using the ESP BLE Mesh beta, we were greeted by a “demo” containing 400+ lines of code; much of it using Macros and functions that I couldn’t find easily, and all of it written in C, a language with which I have had very little experience. Needless to say, getting our feet wet with this program was a bit of a struggle, and I’ve spent much of this week tediously searching through files and API Reference documents trying to find information about how the “demo” program worked and adding comments in order to try and understand the program better. Thankfully, at the end of it all I feel quite confident in my understanding of how the BLE Mesh works with the ESP32, and what we want to do with it shouldn’t be too hard, (keep an eye out on our GitHub Page in the coming week).

Obviously this week I’ve been very focused on BLE Mesh; but despite this I’ve still managed to make some progress in a few other areas. I specifically have been thinking a lot more about the hardware but have ended up with more questions than answers. While attempting to make the above demo work with RGB LEDs strips, I realised that the ESP32 can’t output enough power to make the LEDs work, something that I would have noticed had I simply taken the time to look at the LEDs which are clearly marked with “12v” which is a bigger number than the “3v” printed on the ESP32.

The above demo also demonstrates something less obvious – the case for using MOSFETs over other transistors in our glowstick devices to control the LEDs. As you can see in the demo above, the green LEDs (which are connected with a MOSFET) are much brighter than the red ones. Choosing a type of transistor to control LEDs is neither an interesting nor very hotly contested topic, even by my standards as someone who once read an entire insurance policy from cover to cover for recreation. Instead, the short and simple version is that we need a fancy electronic switch to control our lights, and MOSFETs are better and will make the lights brighter (and as computer scientists MOSFETs are our friends because they helped us kick off this whole microprocessor thing and gave us laptops, smartphones, and hopefully smart glowsticks).

Overall where we stand with hardware now is that the ESP32 can provide the BLE connectivity, the Wi-Fi connection (which we might use in the speaker hubs) and do most of the “computer-y” stuff like deciding on what song to play and handling data. But we will probably need an additional daughterboard to provide audio output via 3.5mm jack, a 12v circuit to run the RGB LEDs (including some MOSFETs), some batteries to keep it all powered on the go, a charging circuit to keep the batteries charged and happy, and something to “step down” the 12v to the correct voltage for the ESP32 which I believe is 5v. Additionally, we’ll need a potentiometer to figure out music selections from a rotating knob or section on the glowstick, and a few other bits and bobs such as a few extra LEDs to help us identify when a device is throwing errors as it isn’t practical to have them connected to a computer to detect errors. This all sounds like a lot of different pieces, but I’m hoping in the coming week to start prototyping this hardware much more intensively and hopefully get it mostly nailed down in the next week or two, and I have a few tricks up my sleeve to get it all rolled into a neat little package, but you’ll have to wait and see.
