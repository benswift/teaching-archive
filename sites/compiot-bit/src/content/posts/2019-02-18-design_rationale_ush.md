You know that feeling when you're at a live music gig and the crowd collectively starts pounding their firsts in the air to the beat of the kick drum as the song builds to a climax? Well,I'm a big fan of that feeling. It got me thinking whether a similar type of energy can be generated by reversing the direction of influence in this setting. So I developed a 'smart bracelet' with the capability of collecting accelerometer and gyroscope data which would be used to change the tempo and volume in a live coding gig. I intend for these devices to lead to richer interactions between an audience and the live music, as well as richer interaction between individual members of the audience. They go by the name of _beatclays_. A live gig with beatclays encourages audience members to experiment with movement and see how their collective motion affects the music.

### Crowd-Surfing on Shoulders of Giants

There's some cool work already being done in the Internet of Musical Things miscrocosm which centres around audience interaction in live music; some of which I found before I began my project and some during. Several of my early findings involved interactions through smart phones. [Open Symphony](http://java.sys-con.com/node/37307) distributes control over the structure of a live piece of music to audiences' phones and [a.bel](https://www.academia.edu/37705759/Bridging_the_gap_between_performers_and_the_audience_using_networked_smartphones_the_a.bel_system) allows musicians to distribute interactive components to audience phones. But I wanted my interaction to be more primal than moving sliders on a touch screen. So, I decided to use motion as the means for interaction since it's a natural response to music, it would be more engaging. At this stage I was inspired and ready to create a device which drives musical parameters or uses patterns in motion data to add rhythmic components to the music. My excited little fingers flicked (clicked) through pages of work like [EyesWeb](http://www.infomus.org/eyesweb_ita.php), [Lightning](https://ipfs.io/ipfs/QmXoypizjW3WknFiJnKLwHCnL72vedxjQkDDP1mXWo6uco/wiki/Buchla_Lightning.html) and [Experio](http://www.nime.org/proceedings/2014/nime2014_481.pdf); all harnessing motion or position in some way. EyesWeb used computer vision to recognise a dancer's gestures and map them to real-time changes to music. I didn't have the confidence to learn and apply computer vision in 10 weeks, so this was out. Then there's Lightning; a rod embedded with infrared transmitters. Depending on the zone in which the rod is held, various midi tracks and samples can be triggered. Finally, Experio came along. What I liked about Experio was the various effects a person's movements could have on the music e.g. triggering pre-created music tracks or changing parameters of existing music. One common thread between these ideas was that they tended to work best when used by one person at a time or to restricted sets of movements. The more I looked more at the theme '(dis)connecting together', the more important it became to have a device which facilitates interactions with a large group of people; an audience.

### Priorities

I think a lot of the restrictions in the ideas above come from a need to preserve the musicality of the live performance. Even current wireless network protocols have been found to be insufficient for the ultra-low latency and high reliability required for real-time musical interfaces. I wanted to minimise restrictions in the audiences' movement as much as possible with my device, so I chose to use the sensor data to control volume and tempo rather than rhythm or pitch. This way, the musicality isn't affected drastically and in turn, lead to better results with a larger audience. As much as I like the range of interactions Experio provides, I chose to focus my efforts to controlling only two musical parameters due to time pressure, but I see increasing the variety of interactions as an extension to this project.
I would aggregate the signed accelerometer data from each bracelet to change the tempo of the music within a certain range and similarly aggregate the signed gyroscope data to change the volume.
By aggregating the data, individual members will not be able to largely affect the music. The audience would have to move together in synchrony to make significant changes to the music and heighten the energy of the performance. I chose to keep the data signed to encourage synchrony in the audiences' movements which would increase tempo and volume. I initially didn't like the fact that having signed data could reduce the tempo and volume if movements were synchronised, but in opposing directions. But, then I thought this was a great opportunity to engage with the '(dis)' part of '(dis)connecting together' theme. Interactions where the audience work against each other would be just as interesting as those where they'd have to work together. It would also give the audience more room to experiment and discover how their movements affect the music. However, I made the accelerometer data unsigned since it would be counterintuitive that accelerating quickly in a 'negative' direction slows the tempo. My aim here is to have the interactions reflect the connections audience members have with each other, showing that there is more to the connected system of audience members when they work together as apposed to flailing around with no synchronisation.

### Design Process

I had three components to my project which I created a clear separation between from the beginning.

1. The client side code which would run on the micro controller responsible for reading data from the external accelerometer and gyroscope and sending this to the server.
2. The python process running on the server which would convert the sensor data to parameters which would affect the music.
3. The Extempore extension which will be launched when the live coder begins their set. This is responsible for making changes to the music in real-time using the parameters calculated by the python process. I chose the live coding language Extempore here since I was familiar with the framework so there was less learning overhead.

My approach to the implementation was to code the individual components first and then integrate the components in the end. I chose to do this because I had the least experience with the Arduino hardware I was using, so I wanted to fully program and test them early on in case I would need to order different components. Though this proved useful for the client side code, I got unnecessarily bogged down on the server side code with small details like code structure. Looking back, I realise, once I had written and tested the client side code, I should have connected each of the components together first and tried to achieve end-to-end communication. If there were any problems which would have prevented me from interfacing communiciation between these components, it would take a longer to time to solve than minor structural details.

### Challenges

When it came to choosing the hardware and software tools I would be using, It was more important to me that I chose components which would be well documented, easily accessible and have a some popularity in their use in IoT projects. Because of this, I had to compromise on the compactness of the bracelet. This was also my first IoT project so I chose programming languages and libraries I was familiar with wherever I could. It was important to me to get something up and running as soon as possible or run into problems as soon as possible. There were good and bad sides to this. For example, the first version of my server side code used the python 'threading' library to handle each client connection concurrently. It meant I was able to get end-to-end communciation up and running quickly, but performance could have been improved by using the asyncio library which offers coroutines which are more lightweight.
I had also failed to prioritise scalability with the implementation. Although, its probably easy to critise this oversight in hindsight. My main priorities was to get something working where the accelerometer data changes some parameters in the live code. I should have made notes about points in my code which would affect the scalability. For example, identifying that in some cases the interleaving of the client connection thread operations would cause the server to read multiple messages from one client and deprive the another. Next to designing how to aggregate the data and map it to parameter values, this was the most challenging part of the project for me. I considered synchronising entry into the point where each thread would read data from its client device, but this could lead to cases where threads are delayed far too long creating a large backlog of messaged from their client. I finally chose to have each thread sleep for a random amount of time within a small fixed range. This sleep time suspends the thread allowing other threads to be scheduled leading to fewer cases where the threads are deprived. I am yet to test this, but worst case scenario, I will take the most recent message in case threads are deprived and there is a large backlog of received messages.

### A Changed Outlook on IoT?

I guess my project didn't have me directly work with the well known aspects of IoT like big data, machine learning and cloud computing. But, before I started my project, I was under the impression that only highly sensitive data was worthy of security measures. Through this project I learned that any type of data being transmitted can be used to derive information about the user's behaviour. e.g. the data from this device might be used to keep people dancing at clubs to increase drink sales. The China study trip in general made me aware of how influential this data can be.
I suppose I also had this comical and cold idea that IoT interfaced people with device and dry data. I associated IoT with talking fridges and hollograms which dehumanised interactions involving IoT for me. But, I now see that IoT devices can be use to enhance human to human connection. I hope, in future iteractions of this project (however many it will take) that my device will do just that.
