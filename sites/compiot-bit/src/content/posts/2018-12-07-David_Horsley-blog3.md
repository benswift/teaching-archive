## Humans are weird

A dense orange cloud surrounds me; with every breath I feel the air, thick like soup, burning me inside as it’s drawn down deeper and deeper into my lungs. The streets are chaotic, a sea of people stretches as far as the eye can see; pressed together so tight it’s hard to tell where your body ends and the next begins. In the background furnaces blaze, sparks fly and smoke stacks roar, trucks and bikes carrying unknown goods swerve and honk as they cut through the crowds. You try and speak but your words fall on deaf ears.

It’s dramatic, but if someone had asked me a few weeks ago what China would be like, I’d probably have said something like that. Huge numbers of people, pollution so bad you can’t see and not speaking the language, arriving in China would be like landing on the surface of another planet. I was expecting to be unable to understand anything that anyone said; to find navigating the streets and daily life impossible and everything so hectic and fast paced that even if I did speak Chinese I would still be unable to get around.

One of the big take home lessons from my short time in China has been just how much of our social cues and our understanding of a situation is inferred not through words, but by body language and reading a situation without the need for verbal interaction. I’ve been amazed by how orderly and calm everyone seems, despite the huge numbers of people I’ve seen - especially in situations like boarding the subway when time is of the essence.

This experience has inspired me to think more broadly about how we as humans interact with one another. Especially the ways in which we interact in large groups, where speaking is difficult (and somewhat uncommon between strangers) and where we begin to interact less as individuals and more like a swarm of people - an amorphous blob in which no single person is in control. You don’t have to look too far to see this kind of interaction - it’s common in crowded shopping centres or subway stations, where a mere few people decide to walk travelling in one direction on a certain side and everyone adapts around them. The most obvious example for me recently has been the streets in China, where it seems common for groups of people, bikes (or even cars) to cross the street or cut in front of other cars, but where the power of the group seems to triumph, and everyone adapts to accommodate for the group.

It’s this type of thinking - about groups of people and interaction without talking - that has really shaped my thoughts about my IoT project over the last week. Music is another example of the strange way humans interact without explicit language, and I think it has an incredible ability to affect us all on a very primal level. A simple example came to me just earlier this week, where at our “blog party”, the song Chasing Cars by Snow Patrol came on. The vibe of the room changed immediately – the energetic and chatty atmosphere seemed to disappear, and it felt like whole room started to sink as people fell silent. I’m sure we’ve all experienced the power of an unexpected song choice at one time or another, let alone the emotion and passion that one can feel through a concert or performance, and much of that is communicated without ever speaking a word.

At this point in this blog, giving how much I’ve spoken about humanity, society and music, I wouldn’t blame you for wondering if there’s something in the water (or those Chinese Red Bulls) or expecting me to declare that I’m breaking free from society to live in the forest and become one with nature. But all of this thought about what it means to interact and how we’re influenced by those around us has really helped me to refine, solidify and understand the direction I want to go with my IoT project. The basic idea behind the project is to explore and provide new ways for our physical presence and interaction with one another – who’s around us, their preferences, etc. – to have a tangible impact upon the environment we share. It’s easy to forget sometimes that each and every person around us is living their own life, has their own taste in music and their own likes and dislikes. I hope that by the end of this project I will have created a system which helps us all to stand back and appreciate the people around us, to open up to them and help to redefine how we interact – even if it is only by changing the music you hear.

Running with the theme of “disconnecting together”, I want our music system to be as streamlined and non-technical as possible from the user’s perspective. I want user to be able to select their music tastes, preferably in a tactile way such as turning a knob on a device, and simply forget about the music, putting their faith in the group and allowing themselves to experience new things, knowing that their preferences are being accounted for just as much as everyone else’s, and that the music they’re listening to has been chosen based on what the people around them right now enjoy listening to. I also would like to see music taste displayed prominently; a (very) rough sketch I created with Oliver this week was of a device similar to a glowstick, worn around the neck and displaying a colour based on the genre of music selected by the user. In making this music taste so public, I hope to create a new way of interaction, taking something which is often quite hidden and making it a centrepiece of everyone’s identity. I think it would be interesting to see how this influences social behaviour - for instance, would people be honest about their music taste? Would people join forces with others with similar tastes, hoping to have their type of music played in a certain area? These are the kind of questions I hope can be raised (but perhaps not answered) by this project.
