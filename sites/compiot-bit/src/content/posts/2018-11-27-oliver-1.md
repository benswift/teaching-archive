# Ep 01 - Abroad
Hi I'm Oliver, born in Melbourne and moved to the ANU to study Advanced Computing and Commerce. Going into the third year I'm trying to find a (business) problem that can be solved with IoT. This post will be the first in a series documenting my travels in China and the ideation process around the artefact.

My only experience of china is through the lens of the Australian media, hardly a paragon of integrity and objectivity. Rabblerousing about the decreasing personal freedoms within China and it's aggressive economic and military attitudes, the Australian/Western international media are my few sources of information on China,I'm interested in getting a proper understanding of the domestic politics within china, and how much it really impinges on the day to day life of her citizens.

Aside from politics, excitement has grown for me about the possibility to use IoT to solve some great problems. It's artefacts transecends all indutries, sectors and demographics; providing for some very intersting and oblique data sets. With this comes security risks and this distributed data requires adequte protections. That being said my only experience with IoT has been COMP2300 - Computer Organisation and Program execution where we spent the semester tinkering on the ST MicroElectronics Discoboard.

## Finding a problem
Having competed in ANU hosted hackathons and industry competitions over the last couple of years, and winning first place in a number of them. A key tool has been to approach issues with the mantra **Person, Problem, Solution** - think about the users, often they can inspire problems and they are the customers to whom will eventually use your solution. With this in mind I've taken two approaches to ideation:

1. An analog process that can become digitized, or can provide insightful data
2. A form of distributed artwork that could perhaps make people aware of the data collected by IoT devices

## A lesson in cyber security
Landing in Hong Kong I had an eight hour layover before my connecting flight to Beijing. Between meals I spent time strolling through the cathedral like terminal. Every third gate ASUS had installed a hub of four devices, two tablets and two notebooks, all of which had administrative level access. One could touch whichever setting they desired, download any app or file, visit any website; basically treat the tablet as their own except of the fact it couldn't leave the dock. And make themselves welcome they did. The first tablet I opened had open Messenger and Gmail accounts, as well as minutes from a meeting for an Indian energy conglomerate.

I spent the next four hours hopping from device to device travelling an estimated 60 gates. I had access to the private email, messenger and photos of 30 people. I saw passports, resumes, corporate investments, government minutes and private photos belonging to complete strangers who had forgotten to log out of public computers.
