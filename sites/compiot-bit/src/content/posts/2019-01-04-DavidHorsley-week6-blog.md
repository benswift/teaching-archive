## The most exciting package of the year

It’s overused, but I think Muhammad Ali was onto something when he said what is perhaps his most famous quote - “The fight is won or lost far away from witnesses - behind the lines, in the gym, and out there on the road, long before I dance under those lights”.
While not strictly relevant to software development, that quote feels apt when talking about my progress on this project over the last week. There’s nothing incredible to show off or talk about, and I still feel a long way off from a finished project. But this time has been of great value; I’ve made significant progress in understanding the project, how it will work and what libraries and frameworks to use, and having done that work I feel more equipped to tackle the real challenges in the weeks ahead. In that way, I hope that this is a week which helps us to “win the fight” in this project, and that at the end we will look back and appreciate how valuable the time spent doing uninteresting, hard work was after all.

As per our schedule from my last blog post, most of the time since the last blog post has been spent doing research in order to get us up and running as soon as possible once our hardware becomes available. Generally speaking I think this time was very successful, but it wasn’t without hiccups. One of the things we needed to decide upon was which framework or platform to use to write and deploy our code, and there were two main options: Arduino or the ESP-IDF (Espressif IoT Development Framework).
Arduino’s advantages are that it’s much easier to understand as it’s intended to be easy-to-use; it has a very large community with a huge number of forums, blogs and projects doing all kinds of neat things, and it’s something I already have some experience with due to it being used for my at-home 3D printer.
The major disadvantage of Arduino over the ESP-IDF is that Arduino lacks some features that exist in the ESP-IDF, owing to the fact that the ESP-IDF is developed directly alongside the ESP hardware, and can therefore be tailor-made to fully utilise the ESP hardware. Unfortunately, for our project there doesn’t seem to be Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) Mesh support in Arduino, which means that at this stage it’s looking like we will need to use the ESP-IDF for this project.

This past week or so has also been a time when I’ve been thinking more about the hardware requirements of our project. A few things came up which in hindsight seem like tremendous oversights considering we haven’t mentioned them much. Specifically, what hardware will be used to actually connect speakers (or possibly headphones) to our devices, and how users will actually physically select their music preferences. The speaker connection seems reasonably easy; there are examples online though it may be necessary to purchase another board which can amplify the signal, and it would require use of an ancient technology known as the “3.5mm headphone jack”. However, there are also examples of using classic Bluetooth to transmit audio, which does avoid the need for any wires but might be complicated if the system were to be used with many devices, as having many devices could result in some interference and it could be difficult to know which device is which.
Thinking about how users can select their music tastes, I’ve decided that at least initially the best option is a knob or portion of the glowstick which can be rotated to adjust the selection. This fits in with my ideas of making the glowsticks themselves easy-to-use and appearing “low tech”, as well as being reasonably simple to implement. A quick visit to Jaycar and $3 later I have found one possible way to so this: a rotary-switch, which has 12 different contacts which could be wired to GPIO pins on the board and used to determine which selection the user has made. The problems I can see with this are that it’s limited to a certain number of options (although 12 genres of music is likely to be enough), and that connecting each contact on the switch to a specific pin on the board would take up a lot of pins, leaving only a few remaining if this switch were connected in this way. Another option I considered - but am not sure about in terms of actual attainability - is using a rotary potentiometer to provide a variable amount of resistance, which can be measured and used to determine how far the dial has been turned, and can in turn be used to determine the user’s music selection.

Perhaps the most important news since the last blog post was completely out of my hands - the arrival of three ESP32 Development boards. It was most exciting package I’ve received all year! The boards arrived yesterday, and I’ve spent much of today getting the ESP-IDF setup and working on my computer, and successfully flashed the classic “Blink” project to one of my boards. Unfortunately, I also spent a good portion of the day looking at the BLE Mesh examples that Espressif have released, which come in at a whooping 400-500 lines of code each, and being relatively new software and still in Beta the BLE Mesh for ESP doesn’t have a lot of documentation. With that said, I do feel like I’m making progress towards understanding how BLE Mesh works and I think it’s achievable for our project. With any luck, now armed with our long-awaited hardware it won’t be long before we have a basic prototype up and running.
