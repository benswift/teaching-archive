# Anecdotal Evidence
Having failed to sit for [Aguste Rodin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thinker) and ponder the scope and scale of IoT devices, instead the experiences and discussions on the trip with peers have really started to drive conversations around possible project ideas.

## 1. Music
Between booking flights, packing bags and the hustle and bustle of travelling overseas I had forgotten to have my hair cut. After a long and cold Canberra winter I was shaggy and forlorn, looking for something fresh. A few days after settling into BIT I wandered down to the local barber and tried my best to ask for a short back and sides. In between snippets of English and butchered Mandarin pulsed a thick bass track that the barber was bopping along to. I tried my best to ask him about the local dance scene and he was more than happy to share (shihunanli), some of the more famous clubs and music bars.

Away from the thrill and embarrassment of trying to get my hair cut, the heavy bass brought the mornings IoT lab to the front, and after some ideation with [David Horsely](LINK) we had some scope of a plan:

A device for silent discos that could personalise music tastes to different areas on the dance floor. As partygoers entered the club they could select different “Glowsticks” that represented different genres or BPM of music. Depending on how people congregated a local sensor array could ping these to Glowsticks to form a heat map of different tastes. The data nerd inside me thinks it would make a great post on  [r/Data_Is_Beautiful](http://www.reddit.com/r/Data_Is_Beautiful) would like this, but it goes further. Assuming that the genres were not too different the heatmap could be used to curate different playlists for different areas based on the ratio of density of different genres.

It could also be used for visual effects, e.g projecting the map onto the ground or using different density areas for different lighting effects.

At a broader scope the “Glowsticks” could also be used by architects to track how people move through a room, by marketers to see how people of different opinions or dispositions may congregate. An interesting experiment would be to invite 100 different people to a dinner party and see how they interact and move throughout the evening.
## 2. Culture Shock
Touching down to a very hazy Beijing it immediately became apparent that a filtered face mask would be mandatory for some of the worse days. As it turns out Fine Particulate Matter is considered [carcinogenic to humans](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3975184/) with many other associated [health issues](https://www.epa.gov/pm-pollution/health-and-environmental-effects-particulate-matter-pm). Coming from cleaner skies, not being able to see more than 200m due to the haze is quite unsettling.

It would be an interesting task to build a device to track how much dust and grit passes through one’s mask through the day. This data could then be sent to one’s phone to get a real time indication of the air quality. If this could be done, each air-quality datapoint could be coupled with the location data of the phone. Finally, I would like to see an online map that could plot out, in realtime, global air quality.

Inspired by a citizen activist who built their own [air monitor](https://www.instructables.com/id/IoT-Air-Quality-Sensor/), the trust deficit in China and other nations could let people hold their environmental regulatory bodies to account through the small feat of check the air quality and comparing it to the official published figures

Some of the hardware that I’ve looked out is [here](http://wiki.seeedstudio.com/Grove-Gas_Sensor-MQ2/), however it may be difficult to find a sensor cheap, small and precise enough for this task.

## 3. Personal Privacy
Data privacy is a personal right. One is made acutely aware of the rise of the surveillance state here in China where almost everywhere you look you will see at least one camera bearing down on you. However, it is not limited to China, worldwide there has been a trend post 9/11 of erosion of civil liberties and personal privacy. Sinisterly this has been done in mostly small steps that, which can covertly condition one to accept these new reduced liberties. The first step to stop this process is to become aware that it is happening. By being aware of what data is being used and by who, people can start to become more engaged in privacy and hopefully stop the march by many governments to implement systems of control under the guise of “National Protection”.

A lot of companies do not let you view the data that you have collected on them. A tool for use around the home that could ping an entire network and see what data is being sent and from/to whom. This tool could display smart data (if unencrypted), or alert users to smart devices that are not disclosing data on you and perhaps suggest alternative providers. If a device is not pushing out data, a local sensor can observe and record the device’s network interactions to try and collect some of that data or infer the type of data it may be collecting.

For completeness the observer could also note where the data was being sent so that one could know who else was prying into your private, home data.
