Z-Wave, ANT and ZigBee might sound like poorly-named supervillains, but they are in fact something much more intimidating: wireless communication protocols. After my last blog post, which was high in touchy-feely human talk but a bit lacking in technical discussion, I decided that this week it was time to get stuck into the nitty-gritty of my project by diving into the world of wireless communication.
Wireless communication is something that I never really put that much thought into. As far as I was concerned, the different standards and protocols were more or less the same thing. Sometimes it’s called Bluetooth and other times it’s called WiFi but the general concept is the same: electromagnetic waves travel through the air, carrying with them the precious 1’s and 0’s that make the internet work. (End blog post)

Unsurprisingly, it wasn’t that easy; in fact, it wasn’t easy at all. I was somewhat aware of a few basic wireless communication protocols, Wifi, Bluetooth (and Bluetooth LE), and some of the more IoT focused protocols such as ZigBee. But I’d never actually sat down and tried to compare them, and pretty quickly I discovered there are in fact important differences between the different protocols, and choosing one is likely to be an important step in the success of my project overall.
I came across 5 possible choices: ANT, Z-Wave, Bluetooth, Bluetooth LE and ZigBee; and [Wikipedia](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ANT_(network)>) has a handy table comparing the differences between them on the page about ANT. It’s pretty clear that despite being very similar in some ways, the differences between them can be very important and before jumping in and committing to any one protocol it’s important to decide what exactly it will be used for. Even for something simple like the transmission range, the differences between the protocols becomes clear. ANT for example, has a range of only 30 metres – hardly enough to reach across the best raves and parties without doing some fancy mesh networking stuff.
It was pretty clear then, that before making any choices about a wireless communication protocol it was necessary to think about how the devices will be used, how they will interact and also consider the availability of hardware, software, community support and documentation for the different protocols.

Unsurprisingly, Ben and Kieran had some good advice about where to start with all of this and pointed me towards the ESP32 from Espressif Systems. The ESP32 has a lot going for it; it’s cheap, has lots of pins for I/O and other features, it’s reasonably powerful and comes with WiFi and Bluetooth support (both legacy and Bluetooth LE). Additionally, some of the ESP32 development boards have battery charging systems built in, allowing for the use of the batteries or USB power, enabling us to rapidly build and test the wearable “glowstick” devices without the need to do much soldering. Another related aspect of choosing a wireless communication system is power consumption, which is important for the wearable glowstick devices. Thankfully, the nice people at [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/power-consumption-analysis-of-bluetooth-low-energy-zigbee-and-ant-sensor-nodes-in-a-cyclic-sleep-scenario/) have looked into this, and according to their study which compared Bluetooth Low Energy, ZigBee and ANT; Bluetooth LE had the lowest power consumption of the three.
We have also been thinking more about the actual use-cases of our project – what it needs to do to be considered successful, and what are some ways we can extend the project in interesting or fun ways.
The most basic version of this project is as follows: a number of wearable devices (notionally called “glowsticks”) broadcast a selected genre of music to a central speaker-hub, which then uses this information to select music. The glowsticks glow in a certain colour based on the music genre selection from the user and are worn around the neck or some other prominent location to make the user’s music selection very prominent. One extension would be to apply some voting or feedback system, allowing users to respond to particular songs being played either positively or negatively, and this feedback would be sent back to the speaker node to modify the music being played. Another possible extension is to allow the glowsticks not only to be used for influencing the music, but actually playing the music either through inbuilt speakers or through a headphone jack, to which the user connects headphones and therefore allows the devices to be used for “silent discos”. Additionally, this independent music functionality allows for some possibly interesting or fun uses, such as giving pairs or groups of people the same music and making a game out of trying to find the other people receiving the same music.
Essentially, the project can be broken down into a number of stages: Stage 1 involves one-directional communication of music preferences from the glowsticks to the speaker-hub, Stage 2 requires some bi-directional communication, with votes being sent back to the speaker-hub from the glowsticks, and Stage 3 includes streaming of music to the glowstick-nodes, possibly including streaming multiple different “channels” of music, each to a specific subset of devices. It is also possible that we may implement lighting, displays or some other visually appealing functionality to make the glowsticks more interesting and engaging, and this may require more data be communicated, depending on how this is implemented.

With a wireless communication protocol and board decided upon (at least as a starting point), I’m hoping it won’t be too long until I can start getting my hands dirty and actually start making things. I would also hope that by next week we will have figured out how to find the location of the glowsticks relative to one-another, which is still something we haven’t fully worked out.
