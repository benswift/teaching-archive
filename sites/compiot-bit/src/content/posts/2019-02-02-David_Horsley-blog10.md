## Old friends and new friends

Three years ago, Dick Smith Electronics made announced that they would be closing more than 300 stores across Australia and New Zealand and that almost 2,500 people would lose their jobs during the closure. It was hardly surprising; their share price had been falling for years and customers complained about high prices and product ranges that didn’t really fit with their needs. 
I visited many times in the weeks and months that followed, as the stores slowly emptied of products and people. There were a few good deals to be had, like a $3 pair of headphones continued to work respectably until this year, and the stores had a certain dystopian, late-stage capitalist feel about them that I found rather interesting. It seemed almost everything was for sale. At my local store, the manager’s desk and chair had been pushed out from the back onto the shop floor and had price tags on them; a collection of used office stationery (which presumably sat on the desk just days earlier) were also for sale, and a mop and bucket still filled with opaque grey water sat in the corner brandishing a freshly applied price tag matching those found on the shelves, the security gates, the cash register and boxes of Dick Smith branded plastic bags – “Own a piece of history!” read a makeshift sign.
I think the reason I kept coming back to those stores during their final days wasn’t about getting good deals or seeing the realities of the final days of a dying business, it was because in a way it felt like I was losing an old friend. 
As a child, there was little else as exciting as hearing my mum announce she was going to Aldi, and while I love Aldi (having purchased everything from drones to rock climbing gear from there over the years), it wasn’t Aldi I was excited about. What I was excited about what the Dick Smith’s Powerhouse located next door to Aldi. The Powerhouse brand was a little different from their regular stores – less computers and consumer electronics, more hobbyist electronics and CB radios. Aisle after aisle filled with ariels and amateur radio supplies, hand tools, soldering irons and power supplies and hundreds and hundreds of plastic drawers holding unrecognisable components like a tiny mausoleum. 
It was this store that I think got me interested in electronics, and over the years I built several projects including an FM radio, a robot that followed black lines, RC vehicles and countless devices that could best be described as “things with LEDs”. 
On weekends, the store would be filled with people picking up parts for their projects, often excitedly telling the store staff about the project as they darted around helping customers locate obscure parts or tools. There was one section of the store, about 1m long and occupying the bottom 3 shelves as I recall, which was seen as the most serious stuff for DIYers: the PCB etching materials. 
In recent years, PCB etching has become something of a lost art; the proliferation of cheap(er) professional PCB etching and international shipping has made homebrew PCBs a bit of a niche, and the “one size fits all” microcontrollers modules are making it easier for people to get into electronics without ever needing to handle individual components. 
Despite this, there are still some people devoted to making their own PCBs, and there have even been some advances in recent years, newer techniques that are (supposedly) easier or require less hazardous chemicals, and the growth of surface-mounted components rather than through-hole removes the need to painstakingly drill holes through custom boards. 
With all of this in mind, I decided earlier this week to attempt making my own PCB. It wasn’t entirely just a challenge, after getting the code for the BLE mesh mostly working earlier this week, the next most important step for the glowsticks was to get the few bits of hardware in them working. The parts that are needed are the RGB LEDs to make them glow in a colour representing the genre selected by the user, a potentiometer to help the user make this selection, and a few bits and pieces like transistors to let the ESP32 control the 12v power required by the LEDs. 
All things considered, it’s a pretty simple circuit if it’s just taken at this depth. But since the ESP32 has many, many more pins than what would be required for this, I decided to “break out” these pins onto the board to, given us a place on the PCB to affix components in the future if we had the time and effort to do so. 
The problem with this is that the ESP32 has a lot of pins, my board has 18 along each side on a board that’s only a little bigger than my thumb. I wanted my board to be similar to most of the “daughter boards / hats” that are available for the most popular microcontrollers such as the Arduino of the Raspberry Pi. These are boards which have female pins that correspond to the location of all of the male pins on the main board, and use these pins for both power and data, and also for support to hold the daughter board in place. What you end up with is a sort of sandwich, with the main board on the bottom, and the daughter board on the top. The result is a very compact arrangement of the two boards, which given the space requirements inside the glowstick this seemed like a good idea. But the small size is a double-edged sword, because it meant limited space on my board for the components and for the ‘traces’ themselves, the small copper lines that act as conductors to make the circuit work. 
As such, I set out to design a board which was more or less the same size as my ESP32 board, with the pins my board matching the exact location of the ones of the ESP32. Having never designed a PCB before, I didn’t really have any idea where to start, but after a bit of research I found myself at [EasyEDA,](https://easyeda.com/) a website that allows for circuit simulation and PCB design in a browser. Within a few hours, I had the circuit sketched out as both a circuit schematic and a PCB layout; the difference being that the circuit schematic simply shows all the components and their connections, while the PCB layout show how all of these components could be combined onto a printed circuit board with enough space for all the components and connections between them. 
With my PCB layout complete, it was time to get making it into an actual PCB. Most of the time that way this work is as follows: you buy a board which is a few millimetres thick, with copper on one or both sides, and fibreglass or some other non-conductive material in the middle. You then cover up the parts of the copper that you wish to keep, where all the pads and traces are to connect the components are, and the rest of the copper plating is removed, usually with a chemical solution. The major difference between the different DIY methods of making PCBs is how you mask the areas of the board you wish to keep. It is possible to transfer an image directly from a printout from a standard laser printer, by applying heat or a combination of chemicals to dissolve the toner in the ink, remove it from the paper it was printed on and get it to set onto the copper board in a process known as “toner transfer”. 
However, I purchased a PCB etching kit from Jaycar which included a number of blank copper boards for use with toner transfer, but also a board treated with a photoresist layer. Essentially, the way the photoresist process works is that each board has a layer on it which reacts to UV light. The PCB layout is printed into transparent film, which is then placed upon the board and the board is exposed to UV light. The printed parts of the transparent film will block the UV light, and these parts of the board remain unexposed. After the exposure to UV light, the board is placed into a developer chemical, which removes either the exposed or unexposed portions of the photoresist layer. After this process, what’s left is a copper board, with some parts of the board protected by the developed photoresist. This board can then be placed into an etchant, which in my case was sodium perchlorate, and the parts of the copper that were not protected by the photoresist are removed. Finally, the end result is a board that has the exact same layout that was initially printed onto the transparent film but etched onto the copper layer on the board itself., which can then be drilled and have components attached. 
Over the course of the last week, I tried both the toner transfer method and the photoresist method, and only got decent results from the photoresist method. Unfortunately, although the final board was a surprisingly good quality, with some of the very fine features appearing clearly on the final board, I had no success in actually getting the components soldered onto this board. The reason for this is twofold; firstly, I was using a lead-free solder which I’ve never used before and had trouble making this stick to the board, and also that I didn’t “tin” the board immediately after etching, which allowed time for an oxide layer to develop on the freshly etched PCB, which makes soldering much more difficult. 
It probably goes without saying that I also picked up many, many other little things during this process, and I think that I could do it again successfully in much less time now that I have some idea of what I’m doing. Hopefully, now in the final weeks of this project we can mostly forget about the hardware, knowing that we can get it working reasonably easily now, and focus instead on getting our software all combined and working nicely. 
Dick Smith’s Electronics is gone, but this week, as I visited Jaycar several times to pick up bits and bobs, I realised how much it brought me back to those days as a child when I would excitedly wander the aisles of Dick Smith’s. While Dick Smith’s might be gone, as long as there are people who making things, breaking things, and learning along the way, I think stores like DSE or Jaycar will continue, even if we have to say goodbye to one every now and then.
